# The AI Bubble: A Useful Tool at an Impossible Price

*Why I think we're due for a significant correction — even though the underlying technology is genuinely useful.*

*Published: June 9, 2026*

Most arguments about an "AI bubble" go off the rails in the first thirty seconds, so I want to be clear about what I'm not claiming.

I don't think large language models are a parlor trick. I use them every day, and for some work (particularly writing code) a fast first draft you can check and fix is worth real money. But it pays to be precise about what kind of tool this is. An LLM is probabilistic. It predicts plausible text; it doesn't actually know anything in a grounded sense. And it matters, because the whole valuation case rests on the tool being something it still isn't: dependable enough to run unsupervised across a wide range of tasks.

My claim is narrower than "AI is fake," and I think it's the one people should be worried about: **the tool is useful, but the valuations and cost structures built on top of it assume a level of reliability and economic return disconnected from all current evidence and realistic projections.** The internet was real too, and that didn't stop the companies built on it from losing something like 80% of their market value between 2000 and 2002. Pets.com was a bad business wrapped around a real revolution. You can be completely right that a technology is useful and completely wrong about the price.

The price is the problem. Seven things convince me of that, and they come at the valuations from four directions: the technology's own limits, the economics, a physical buildout that may not arrive on time, and a financing structure that could turn a correction into something systemic. Together they've quietly made this almost everyone's bet, whether they realize it or not. The first one bounds all the rest, so that's where I'll start.

---

## 1. Hallucination is built into how the tool works, and it caps what the valuations assume

The most important fact about LLMs as a *business* is that they confidently produce false information, and after many years and tens of billions of dollars, nobody has solved it. Reduced it in narrow domains, yes; solved it, no. And the reason may be that it can't be solved within this paradigm at all.

This is no longer a skeptic's talking point. In September 2025, **OpenAI's own researchers**, together with Georgia Tech, published a paper arguing that hallucinations are *mathematically inevitable* — a structural consequence of how these models are trained and evaluated, not an engineering defect that better data or a bigger model will sand away. Their framing is that the training and evaluation procedures literally reward confident guessing over admitting uncertainty: like a student on an exam, the model is incentivized to produce a plausible answer rather than say "I don't know." A complementary line of work points at the transformer architecture itself, describing these systems as "coherence engines" optimized for statistical plausibility rather than truth, with no built-in mechanism to ground a claim in verifiable fact. When the company with the most to lose from this conclusion is the one publishing it, you should take it seriously.

This single property is what bounds the entire bull case. The valuations are not priced on "a useful coding assistant that a developer double-checks." They are priced on autonomous agents replacing knowledge workers, on AI handling customer money, legal filings, medical triage, and back-office operations with little human in the loop. Every one of those applications requires the model to be *trustworthy without supervision* — and a tool that confidently asserts false things at an irreducible rate cannot be trusted without supervision. The supervision is the cost. It is the human who has to stay in the loop, check the output, and carry the liability. That human is exactly the cost the valuations assume gets eliminated.

This is why I am bullish on LLMs for code and bearish on the valuations in the same breath. Code generation is the *best* case for the technology precisely because it has a cheap, fast verification layer: the code either compiles and passes tests or it doesn't, and a developer is already in the loop to review it. The domains that would justify trillion-dollar outcomes are the ones *without* that cheap verification layer — the ones where a confident wrong answer is expensive, sometimes catastrophic, and hard to catch. The technology is most valuable exactly where it is easiest to check, and least trustworthy exactly where the money is. Until hallucination is solved (and its own creators now suggest it cannot be), that ceiling holds, and everything that follows below sits underneath it.

## 2. The base models are improving more slowly than the headlines suggest

Over the last two years, **most of the visible improvement has come from the scaffolding around the model, not from the model itself.**

When GPT-4 arrived in 2023, it was a step-change in the raw capability of the underlying LLM. What has happened since has been different in kind. The big jumps (agentic workflows, tool use, "reasoning" via chain-of-thought, retrieval-augmented generation, test-time compute where the model is allowed to think longer) are overwhelmingly improvements in the *harness* wrapped around the LLM. We got better at prompting the model, letting it call tools, letting it check its own work, and letting it burn more compute at inference time. The weights at the center of all this are improving far more slowly than they were.

For a hands-on version of this, try it yourself. Ask the Claude chat app to write a complex, multi-file program, then ask Claude Code to build the same thing. The underlying model is identical. The chat app hands you a block of code that you then have to copy out, run, and debug yourself, and on anything non-trivial it usually takes a few rounds of pasting errors back before it works. Claude Code closes that loop on its own: it reads your files, runs the code, sees the compiler errors, and fixes them before you ever see the output — often landing on working software in one pass. Nothing about the model changed between the two. The harness did the work, and from the outside it looks like the intelligence got smarter.

The scaling laws that everyone cited in 2021 (double the compute, reliably get a better model) have flattened where it counts. In 2021, doubling pre-training compute bought you a roughly proportional jump across most benchmarks. By 2025, the same doubling buys something like a 10–20% improvement on the hardest reasoning tasks, with sharply diminishing returns past a threshold. Knowledge benchmarks like MMLU plateau beyond ~30B parameters; reasoning benchmarks like GSM8K plateau around 70B+. You can keep climbing, but each rung costs exponentially more and lifts you less.

This is not a fringe view held by skeptics. Ilya Sutskever (a co-founder of OpenAI and one of the people most responsible for the scaling paradigm in the first place) said flatly at NeurIPS in December 2024 that *"pre-training as we know it will end,"* and that the bottleneck has shifted from compute to data. He has every incentive to talk the technology up, too: he testified in court that his OpenAI stake alone is worth roughly **$7 billion**, on top of the AI lab he now runs. When the person who helped build the engine tells you the engine is approaching its design limit, that is worth more than a hundred bullish analyst notes.

Even the way GPT-5 landed in August 2025 tells the story. It was more capable than GPT-4. But ask anyone who used both: the *felt* gap between GPT-4 and GPT-5 was much smaller than the gap between GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. That is exactly what a maturing technology on the flat part of an S-curve feels like. The marketing says "exponential." The user experience says "we are climbing the ceiling, not raising it."

This matters for valuation because the entire bull case is priced on the assumption that the curve keeps bending upward — that next year's model is to this year's what GPT-4 was to GPT-3. If progress is instead asymptotic in the base model and incremental in the harness, then the "we'll grow into the valuation" argument loses its engine. Harness improvements are real, but they are also far easier to copy, which brings me to the moat problem later.

## 3. There are strong reasons to doubt this path leads to AGI — and the data well is running dry

Look at what these valuations actually require. OpenAI is priced at $852 billion on roughly $25 billion of annualized revenue, about **34 times sales** for a company still deep in the red. The hole only gets deeper: OpenAI has reportedly committed roughly **$1.4 trillion** to future compute, power, and data-center deals — more than fifty times its current revenue, and money it does not remotely have. Anthropic, on revenue a fraction of its valuation, was reportedly priced near $1 trillion on its last funding round. No productivity tool, however good, earns multiples like these or justifies future commitments like these. What you are really paying for is a call option on AGI: the belief that one of these labs builds something approaching general intelligence and captures a winner-take-all share of essentially all cognitive labor. That is the bet baked into the price, and I think that option is worth far less than the market is paying for it, for a specific and underappreciated reason: **the dominant method for getting here has essentially run out of fuel.**

The method that produced the last decade of gains was conceptually simple — take more high-quality data and more compute and make the model bigger. The compute is still scaling (at ruinous cost, see below). The *data* is not. The frontier labs have, by most credible estimates, already trained on the overwhelming majority of high-quality text humanity has produced: the crawlable web, the digitized books, the code, the academic corpus. You cannot 10x a dataset that already contains most of what exists. This is the "data wall," and it is the real reason Sutskever and others say pre-training is ending. It is not that the recipe stopped working in principle; it is that we ran out of the main ingredient.

The obvious proposed fix (generate synthetic data with LLMs and train the next LLM on it) runs into a wall of its own, and this one has a peer-reviewed name: **model collapse.** A 2024 study in *Nature* by Shumailov, Shumaylov and colleagues demonstrated that when you recursively train generative models on their own output, performance degrades, generation after generation. The models begin by forgetting the tails of the distribution (the rare, the unusual, the long-tail facts) and progressively converge toward a blander, lower-variance, less accurate version of reality. The insidious part is that early collapse is hard to notice: aggregate metrics can even look fine while the model quietly loses its grip on the minority cases that often matter most.

Now layer in the second-order problem. The open internet (the very corpus future models will be trained on) is being flooded with LLM-generated text at industrial scale. Every AI-written blog post, product description, forum answer, and content-farm article is contaminating the well that the next generation of models needs to drink from. We are, collectively, poisoning our own training data. Carefully curated synthetic data with verification (math, code, anything with a checkable ground truth) does help, and has driven some of the recent gains, but it is a narrow, expensive, hand-built process, not the broad data abundance that powered the scaling era. It is not a substitute for a fresh ocean of human knowledge, because no such ocean is coming.

So the path to AGI via "more data, more scale" is, by the admission of its own architects, largely tapped out. The alternative paths (test-time compute, new architectures, agentic systems) are promising research directions, but "promising research direction" is a very different financial object than "reliable scaling law we can extrapolate to a trillion-dollar outcome." You do not get to keep AGI-option pricing while quietly admitting the mechanism that justified it has stalled.

## 4. The frontier labs are burning billions with no durable moat — and the competition is nearly free

This is where the business model, as opposed to the science, falls apart.

**The burn.** OpenAI's own internal documents reportedly forecast a **$14 billion loss in 2026**, with some updated projections putting cash burn far higher — on the order of $27 billion in 2026 rising toward $63 billion in 2027 under renegotiated terms. The company is not expected to be cash-flow positive until around 2030, if then. It is generating roughly $2 billion a month in revenue (about $25 billion annualized), a remarkable figure for a company a few years old, and still losing money at a rate that requires it to raise tens of billions just to keep the lights on. It closed a $122 billion round at an $852 billion post-money valuation in March 2026 and is reportedly seeking another ~$100 billion. Anthropic, the better-run business of the two on a unit basis, has a revenue run-rate that has grown very fast, but its valuation has sprinted even faster, reportedly approaching a trillion.

Spending billions to acquire a market is a fine strategy *if you can eventually raise prices to a profitable level.* That requires a moat. And the frontier labs do not have one that holds.

**The competition is nearly free.** Consider what a moat would have to defend against. The marginal product they sell (intelligence per token) is becoming a commodity, and the commodity is being priced toward zero by competitors who do not need to earn a return on a trillion-dollar valuation. The clearest example is the Chinese open-weight models. DeepSeek's R1, released openly, delivers reasoning performance in the rough neighborhood of frontier Western models at a price that is not a little cheaper but *categorically* cheaper. DeepSeek R1 runs around **$0.55 per million input tokens and $2.19 per million output tokens**; the comparable OpenAI reasoning model was priced at roughly **$15 and $60**. That is on the order of **27 times cheaper** — DeepSeek costs a few percent of the incumbent. And because the weights are open, anyone can self-host them, fine-tune them, and escape API pricing entirely. The smaller open-weight models (and distilled versions of the large ones) can now run on a high-end laptop, with no data-center compute and no monthly bill at all.

This is a catastrophic competitive dynamic for a company that needs pricing power to justify its valuation. If "good enough" intelligence is available at 3–5% of your price, and frequently for free if a customer is willing to run it themselves, then your ability to raise prices is capped not by your costs but by your nearest free competitor. The frontier labs are in the position of a company that spent $50 billion building a slightly better mousetrap, only to find that a perfectly serviceable mousetrap is being given away in the parking lot. The 20% quality edge at the frontier matters enormously for a narrow band of cutting-edge use cases — and not at all for the large majority of commercial workloads, which is where the revenue to justify these valuations would have to come from.

**The Jevons rebuttal.** The smartest bulls turn this around on me. DeepSeek being 27 times cheaper, they argue, isn't the threat — it's the unlock. Drop the price of a token far enough and AI gets poured into everything: every product, every API call, every workflow. Cheap intelligence gets used the way cheap electricity did, and consumption explodes. That's the Jevons paradox, and as a prediction about volume I think it's basically right. The problem is that volume isn't revenue. Usage can rise a hundredfold while total revenue still falls, because in a commoditizing market the price drops faster than the volume climbs. It happened to long-distance minutes, to DRAM, to solar panels, to bandwidth — demand went vertical and most of the companies selling into it went broke anyway, because none of them had pricing power. For Jevons to save these valuations you need two things at once: demand outrunning price, and somebody holding a real margin on all that volume. When the next-best option is open-weight and nearly free, I don't think the labs get both.

The harness improvements I described in Section 2 make this worse, not better. Because so much of the recent capability gain lives in scaffolding (agent frameworks, RAG, tool use, prompting techniques), and scaffolding is far easier and cheaper to replicate than a frontier training run, the durable advantage of being six months ahead on raw model quality keeps shrinking. The thing you can defend (a better base model) is improving slowly; the things that are improving quickly (the harness) are the things you cannot defend.

**The harness tax.** The labs' own pricing behavior gives the game away. If you cannot build a moat around the model, you try to build one around the *harness* — and then you have to force people to use it. Look at what Anthropic did in April 2026: it stopped letting Claude Pro and Max subscribers run their plan through third-party agentic tools. Use Claude through Anthropic's own harnesses (Claude Code, Claude Cowork) and your usage stays inside the flat subscription. Use Cline, aider, Roo Code, OpenCode, or any other front end on the *same underlying model*, and that usage now bills at full per-token API rates on top of the subscription you already pay. Same model, same tokens, materially different price depending on whether you came through Anthropic's wrapper or someone else's. OpenAI, for its part, zigged the other way — unifying its Codex usage and dangling one-click migration plus two months free to poach exactly the power users Anthropic was busy taxing.

The official justification is prompt-cache efficiency, and there is a real technical wrinkle there. But strip away the rationale and look at the incentive: this is a toll on anyone who declines to stand inside the company's own application layer. It is a tacit admission that the model itself is not a moat — if it were, you would not need to penalize people for accessing it through a competitor's interface. You charge a premium for harness-independent usage precisely *because* the model underneath has become a commodity that anyone can wrap. This is rent-seeking: when a company can no longer defend the thing it actually sells (the tokens), it manufactures friction to herd users into its own wrapper and collect a toll the market would never grant it on the merits.

Over the medium and long term, I think this strategy backfires, and badly. Penalizing the open developer ecosystem is a tax on your own distribution — the third-party tools, the indie agents, the experimentation layer are how a model gets embedded into real workflows in the first place. Punish that layer and you push the most price-sensitive, most sophisticated users toward exactly the alternatives that should frighten you most: model-agnostic tooling that makes switching trivial, and the open-weight Chinese models that are already a fraction of the price and that you can wrap in any harness you like, forever, for free. You are trying to build a wall around the application layer at the very moment the commodity underneath it is getting cheaper and more portable. That is not the move of a company with pricing power. It is the move of a company that has discovered it doesn't have any, and is reaching for lock-in to manufacture some. Lock-in extracted by penalty rather than earned by preference is the most fragile kind there is — it raises switching incentives faster than it raises switching costs, and it curdles the goodwill of the developers who are, right now, the only thing keeping these ecosystems sticky. And the timing makes it worse: they are squeezing that ecosystem at the exact moment its developers have the strongest incentive in history to leave, with nearly free open-weight models a one-line config change away. It buys margin this quarter at the cost of the moat next year.

And reality has already stress-tested the premise that the harness is defensible. In March 2026, Anthropic accidentally shipped a source-map file in its public Claude Code npm package, exposing roughly 512,000 lines of the tool's agent harness — the very orchestration layer it is simultaneously charging a premium to keep you inside. Within hours the code was mirrored across GitHub and developers had begun reimplementing the core in other languages. A clone still needs a capable model behind it and a great deal of tuning, so this did not make the harness worthless overnight — but it is a clear demonstration of the point: the scaffolding the industry is treating as a moat was one `.npmignore` typo away from being free, and replicable in an afternoon once anyone could see it. You cannot build durable pricing power on an asset that copies that easily.

The more sophisticated moat play isn't the client wrapper at all — it's building the scaffolding directly into the API: server-side tools, web search, code execution, memory, computer use, and managed agent loops, so that using the model means using the lab's platform. This is their best shot at differentiating above raw tokens, and it deserves to be taken seriously. But it hits the same wall. The connective tissue for all of it — the protocol that lets a model call tools and agents — is MCP, which Anthropic itself created, open-sourced, and in December 2025 handed to a Linux Foundation body that OpenAI co-founded. The agentic plumbing is now an open standard that works with any model, the free open-weight ones included; you can wire DeepSeek into the same tool ecosystem and get most of the same integrated experience. Server-side scaffolding raises switching costs at the margin, but the labs commoditized their own moat the moment they made the standard open.

The demand side gives little comfort either. MIT's Project NANDA study, *The GenAI Divide* (2025), found that despite $30–40 billion in enterprise spending on generative AI, **95% of corporate pilots produced no measurable P&L impact.** The 5% that worked were real and valuable — but a market where nineteen of twenty deployments fail to show a return is not a market about to deliver the explosion of pricing power needed to service these valuations. It is a market still searching for the use cases that justify the spend.

## 5. The infrastructure spend has lapped any plausible return — and it depreciates fast

Even if you reject everything above, this part should give you pause.

The capital being poured into AI infrastructure has detached from any reasonable forecast of the returns it can generate in a competitive market. The four largest hyperscalers (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta), together with Oracle, are on track to spend on the order of **$700 billion** on capex in 2026, up roughly 77% year over year from an already-record 2025. Big Tech AI capex has gone from about $162 billion in 2022 to roughly $448 billion in 2025 to this. Goldman Sachs models a cumulative **$5+ trillion** of hyperscaler capex from 2025 to 2030.

Set that against the revenue side. Sequoia's David Cahn framed this as the "$600 billion question" back in mid-2024: roughly how much annual *end-user* AI revenue the industry would need to generate to justify the infrastructure being built, derived by taking Nvidia's run-rate data center revenue, doubling it for total cost of ownership, and doubling again for a normal gross margin. In late 2023 that number was ~$200 billion. By mid-2024 it was ~$600 billion. At 2026 spending levels it is meaningfully larger still — likely well over a trillion dollars of annual revenue the industry would need to be generating to make the math work. The actual end-user AI revenue being generated is a small fraction of that. The hyperscalers' own AI cloud revenue is real and growing fast, but **none of them have demonstrated positive ROI on their AI infrastructure at scale.** The gap between what is being spent and what is being earned is not closing. It is widening.

Two features make this worse than a normal overbuild.

**First, these are rapidly depreciating assets.** History's great infrastructure bubbles at least left behind something durable. America's 19th-century railroad booms were wild speculative manias that ended in ruin (the overbuilding and railroad-bond frenzy behind the Panic of 1873 bankrupted 89 railroads and roughly 18,000 businesses and tipped the country into a four-year depression), yet the 35,000 miles of track laid in that boom, and the roadbeds, bridges, tunnels, and rights-of-way beneath them, are still carrying freight a century and a half later. The dot-com fiber bust tells the same story: silica doesn't corrode or degrade, so the glass fiber itself can carry light for a century, and even the buried cable around it is rated for 25 to 40 years, which is why much of the "dark fiber" overbuilt in the late 1990s is still in the ground and still in use today. In each case the bubble ruined the investors, but the underlying asset outlived the mania and was eventually put to work. GPUs break that pattern.

Michael Burry's depreciation thesis (whatever you think of the messenger) points at something real: hyperscalers are depreciating AI chips over five to six years, while the economically useful life of a frontier GPU, given how fast Nvidia ships new generations, may be closer to two or three. Burry estimates this understates depreciation by roughly **$176 billion across 2026–2028**, flattering reported profits today at the cost of a reckoning later. There is a legitimate counterargument — a chip can cascade from frontier training down to inference, then to smaller-model serving, then to ordinary cloud work, earning its keep over a longer life, and used A100s have held value better than the bears predicted. But notice what that defense concedes: the *frontier* value of the asset, the value that justified buying it, evaporates in a couple of years. You are building a $700 billion asset base whose cutting-edge earning power has the shelf life of milk.

There is a deeper, less hedgeable version of that obsolescence risk hiding inside the bull case's own logic. The depreciation argument above only assumes today's GPUs get superseded by *newer GPUs* — painful, but you are still buying Nvidia. The sharper danger is that the method which finally breaks the capability plateau does not run on this class of hardware at all. Recall that the bulls concede brute-force scaling is hitting diminishing returns and that further progress will require some algorithmic or architectural breakthrough. But that is precisely the kind of advance most likely to change *what* compute is needed — sparser architectures that don't need dense GPU interconnect, state-space models with different memory profiles, radically more efficient training that collapses the compute required, or eventually non-GPU substrates entirely. If the breakthrough is algorithmic rather than brute-force, the installed base is not upgraded by a better version of itself; it is stranded, because the bottleneck it was built to solve stopped being the bottleneck. There is a quiet contradiction here that the trillion-dollar capex plans never resolve: you cannot simultaneously claim that scaling is exhausted *and* that the right move is to spend five trillion dollars on infrastructure optimized for scaling. And the boom actively funds its own escape hatch — scarcity and cost are the mother of efficiency, which is exactly why a capital-constrained, export-restricted DeepSeek was the one to prove a competitive model could be trained for a fraction of the compute. Every dollar that makes frontier GPUs scarcer raises the bounty on methods that need fewer of them. The fair counterweight is that GPUs are general-purpose enough to run many new methods tolerably, that cheaper effective compute can *raise* total demand (Jevons paradox), and that paradigm shifts take years — so this is a tail risk, not a base case. But it is the tail that turns a slow bleed into a detonator: a method shift that strands the hardware would hit Nvidia's customer concentration, the debt collateralized by those GPUs, and the circular financing all at once.

**Second, a meaningful slice of the apparent demand is circular.** Nvidia invests in OpenAI; OpenAI commits hundreds of billions to cloud providers like Oracle and Microsoft; those providers turn around and buy Nvidia GPUs to fulfill the contracts. By 2026, analyses put the web of these reciprocal commitments north of $800 billion. Money moving in a circle among a small cohort of players inflates the *appearance* of demand and revenue without a proportionate amount of independent, end-customer cash entering the system. This is not a novel pattern. Vendor financing was a hallmark of the dot-com bubble — Cisco financed the very customers buying its gear, and when demand reversed in 2000 it took a then-record $2.2 billion write-down. The circularity does not prove a bubble by itself, but it does mean the demand signal everyone is extrapolating from is partly a hall of mirrors.

The whole structure also narrows to a frighteningly small number of buyers. By Nvidia's own filings, just four direct customers account for roughly **61% of its revenue**, and hyperscalers make up about half of its data-center sales. That is the keystone of the entire edifice: the most valuable company on earth depends on a handful of customers, whose AI capex is discretionary, whose returns on that capex are unproven, and several of whom are tied to Nvidia through the same circular deals described above. If even one or two of those budgets pause (because the ROI does not show up, or because the depreciation reckoning lands), the revenue that anchors the most important stock in the market wobbles, and it does not wobble in isolation.

Add it up: a $700-billion-a-year, fast-depreciating capital build, justified by a revenue base a fraction of the size needed, with a chunk of the demand signal circulating among the same handful of balance sheets, and the linchpin vendor leaning on four customers for most of its sales. That is the setup for a correction — not a verdict on the technology, which provides genuine value, but on the economics behind it, which don't add up.

## 6. Power is a ceiling that money cannot lift on demand

Even if every other objection in this essay were wrong, there is a physical wall the spreadsheets keep waving away: electricity.

The models and the data centers can be built in two to three years. The power to run them cannot. Anthropic itself estimates that training a single frontier model will require around five gigawatts by 2027, and that the U.S. AI sector will need roughly **50 gigawatts of new electric capacity by 2028** to stay competitive — a build-out on the scale of dozens of large power plants, on a deadline. But connecting a new facility to the grid now takes **four to ten years** in much of the country; interconnection-queue wait times have more than doubled over the past fifteen years and average around five years. The compute timeline and the power timeline are simply incompatible, and you cannot pour concrete to fix a transformer shortage or a transmission-permitting backlog. Power, not chips, is becoming the binding constraint — which means a large fraction of the capacity these valuations assume comes online *on schedule* may not.

**Stranded before it earns a cent.** The mismatch has a perverse sting once you recall how short a GPU's useful life is. A frontier chip has perhaps two to three years at the cutting edge (Section 5), but the power to run it can be four or more years away. A chip bought today can therefore spend the most valuable years of its life sitting in a crate waiting for a substation, and reach the plug already a generation or two behind the newest silicon — having earned nothing in the interim. This is not hypothetical: Microsoft's CEO has said the company already has *"a bunch of chips sitting in inventory that I can't plug in,"* calling it "my problem today." A fast-depreciating asset stranded for years before it can earn a cent is close to the worst thing you can buy, particularly with borrowed money.

**A uniquely hostile load.** It is worse than a simple supply shortfall: an AI data center is not just a *big* load, it is a destabilizing one. A traditional data center draws a steady, predictable hum; the grid was built for that. An AI training cluster does the opposite. Tens of thousands of GPUs are deliberately synchronized to compute in lockstep, alternating between a power-hungry computation phase and a quieter communication phase, which means the whole facility's draw can lurch from roughly 30% to 100% and back *in milliseconds* — power swings measured in tens of megawatts per second, and in the largest deployments hundreds to thousands of megawatts within a fraction of a second. To the grid, a gigawatt-scale training run looks like someone switching a mid-sized city on and off several times a minute. That behavior produces voltage sags, frequency oscillations, and harmonic distortion that can ripple out and destabilize the grid for everyone else connected to it; utilities are now explicitly asking hyperscalers to engineer out these "synchronous cycle oscillations" before they will grant interconnection. And the conventional fixes do not fit: diesel generators and gas turbines physically cannot ramp fast enough to catch a millisecond spike, so operators are being pushed toward expensive grid-scale battery buffers just to make the load *tolerable* to the network. None of this complexity, cost, or utility friction is reflected in a slide that simply assumes "we will add 50 gigawatts."

**The equipment backlog.** Then there is the physical equipment that actually moves the electricity, where the bottleneck is unglamorous and binding. A large power transformer (the unit that steps grid voltage down for a data center) used to ship in 7 to 14 months before the pandemic. Lead times are now stretching to **four and five years**, with some specialized units quoted at 36–48 months and beyond. High-voltage substations run 3–5 years. The gas turbines many of these campuses are counting on for dedicated generation are worse still: GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, and Mitsubishi are each sitting on order backlogs that stretch roughly **five years**, with delivery slots already sold into the next decade — Mitsubishi is sold out through 2028. These are not products you can spin up with more capital; they require specialized factories, grain-oriented electrical steel, and skilled labor that took decades to atrophy and will take years to rebuild. The consequence is already visible: industry analyses warn that **more than half** of planned U.S. data centers may be delayed or cancelled — not for lack of money or land, but because the electrical gear to energize them does not exist and cannot be bought at any price for years. When the constraint is a transformer factory's backlog rather than a balance sheet, throwing another $100 billion at the problem does not make the capacity appear. It just bids up the price of a four-year wait.

**Water and cooling.** The physical constraints don't stop with electricity. An AI data center is, at its core, an industrial thermodynamic problem: all that power turns into heat, and the heat has to go somewhere. A single large campus can consume millions of gallons of water a day for evaporative cooling — one Meta site in Newton County, Georgia reportedly draws around 500,000 gallons a day, roughly a tenth of the entire county's water, and newer permits in the state seek up to **6 million gallons a day**. The bigger problem is where this is being built: by one analysis, about **two-thirds of the planned U.S. AI data centers** are going up in areas already under water stress, putting hyperscalers in direct competition with farms and households for scarce groundwater. Newer designs are shifting toward closed-loop liquid cooling, which uses less water, but the heat still has to be rejected somewhere — usually through cooling towers that evaporate it — so the constraint eases rather than disappears.

**A political tripwire.** This demand is already showing up on ordinary people's utility bills and local water tables: households near major data-center clusters in Virginia, Texas, and Georgia have seen electricity rates climb 8–15%, and Dominion filed its first base-rate increase since 1992. Voters do not stay quiet for long when their power bill rises and wells run dry so a hyperscaler can train a model to take their job, and the backlash (moratoria, special data-center tariffs, permitting fights) is a cost and a delay that none of the trillion-dollar TAM slides have priced in. The bull case implicitly assumes the grid and community say yes, on time, at an acceptable political cost. I would not underwrite that assumption.

## 7. The buildout is now financed with debt — which is how a sector correction becomes a systemic one

For most of this cycle, the AI build was paid for with the most comfortable money in the world: the operating cash flow of a few hugely profitable tech companies. That is changing, and the change is the most underappreciated risk in the whole story.

**Onto borrowed money, and off the books.** The arithmetic forced it. AI revenue across the industry was on the order of $60 billion in 2025 against capex of roughly $450 billion, and that gap cannot be filled out of pocket forever. So the spending is increasingly migrating onto borrowed money and, crucially, *off* the balance sheets where investors can see it. By recent estimates, more than **$120 billion of data-center spend has been moved into special-purpose vehicles, private-credit deals, and other off-balance-sheet structures in under two years.** Meta financed its Hyperion campus through a $27 billion private-credit deal with Blue Owl. Oracle's OpenAI buildout sits inside a lattice of SPVs and debt packages — roughly $13 billion from Blue Owl and JPMorgan into one Texas facility, a $38 billion debt package for two more sites, an $18 billion loan for another. Some of the GPUs in this boom are now, quite literally, funded by junk bonds.

**Why debt changes the downside.** It changes the *character* of a downturn. An equity-funded overbuild is painful but self-contained: shareholders lose money, valuations reset, life goes on — that is roughly the dot-com story. A debt-funded overbuild is different. Leverage transmits stress. When the assets backing that debt are GPUs depreciating on the shelf-life-of-milk schedule from Section 5, and the cash flows servicing it depend on AI returns that 95% of enterprise pilots have so far failed to produce, you have built a chain in which distress at any one node (a struggling neocloud, a renegotiated OpenAI contract, a missed Oracle milestone) can propagate to lenders, private-credit funds, and the pensions and insurers behind them. The opacity is the danger: the web of SPVs and off-balance-sheet commitments makes the true leverage in the system genuinely hard to see from outside, and unseen leverage is exactly how a contained sector correction metastasizes into something systemic. This is an old story, not a new one. The same railroad booms that left those durable tracks were financed with so much leverage that their busts cascaded through the banks behind them and tipped the United States into depression twice — in 1873, and again in 1893, when roughly a quarter of the nation's rail mileage ended up in receivership.

**The tech-subprime conduit.** Stack Burry's depreciation point on top of this financing and the picture gets worse. The collateral behind much of this private credit is the GPUs themselves, and those chips shed their frontier earning power in two to three years while the loans written against them often stretch five years or more. When the asset depreciates faster than the debt amortizes, the lender is left under-collateralized against hardware worth a fraction of the loan, repaid out of cash flows from AI demand that may never materialize. That is the same defect that sat under subprime: debt issued against an asset everyone assumed would hold its value, bundled into vehicles opaque enough that the true exposure stayed invisible until it detonated. Only here the collateral is worse. A house in 2007 at least had walls and a lot; a two-generations-old training GPU can be close to worthless. And the lenders are increasingly not the tech giants but private-credit funds, insurers, and the pension money behind them — the conduit through which a data-center write-down becomes a problem for people who have never heard the word "hyperscaler." The part that everyone forgets about 2008 is that the problem was never the houses; it was the financing stacked on top of them.

**The reflexive twist.** All this borrowing competes for capital with everything else hitting the market at the same moment (trillions of new Treasuries funding federal deficits, hundreds of billions of fresh corporate and private credit funding the buildout, and a wave of new equity issuance still to come), and that simultaneous flood pushes the cost of capital up across the board. The trouble is that AI equities are long-duration assets, their value concentrated in cash flows years out, which makes them more sensitive to the discount rate than almost anything else in the market. So the very financing required to keep the build going raises the rate that, applied to those distant cash flows, deflates the valuations the build was meant to justify — the boom, in effect, competing with itself for capital, and worth less the more it has to borrow.

## The clearest tell: a TAM larger than the entire U.S. economy

If you want a single artifact that captures how unmoored the numbers have become, look at the SpaceX S-1, filed May 20, 2026 ahead of a roughly **$1.75 trillion IPO**. At that price the company would trade at about **94 times its 2025 revenue** of $18.7 billion (a multiple at which Alphabet, on its sales, would be worth north of $30 trillion), and Morningstar's own discounted-cash-flow model pegs SpaceX's fair value at roughly $780 billion, less than half the IPO target.

In it, SpaceX tells prospective investors: *"We believe we have identified the largest actionable total addressable market in human history."* They quantify that TAM at **$28.5 trillion**, roughly the size of the entire annual GDP of the United States and larger than that of the EU. Of that $28.5 trillion, only $370 billion is attributed to Space (the actual rockets) and $1.6 trillion to Connectivity (Starlink). The remaining **$26.5 trillion, more than 90% of the claimed market, is AI.** A launch company is anchoring a trillion-dollar-plus IPO on an artificial-intelligence opportunity larger than the economy of the country it's filing in.

Now put the claim next to the company's actual position in that market. The battleground for real AI revenue today is enterprise contracts (businesses choosing which models to build on), and SpaceX's AI arm, xAI, has almost none of it. As of early 2026, Anthropic and OpenAI each held roughly a third of U.S. enterprise AI adoption (about 34% and 32% respectively), Google sat around 5%, and **xAI was under 2%.** Grok, xAI's flagship model, has seen usage decline, struggled with adoption, and watched all twelve of its co-founders depart. Even after folding xAI into SpaceX in February 2026, the combined AI segment booked roughly **$3.2 billion in revenue in 2025 against a ~$6.4 billion operating loss.** The company claiming the largest addressable market in human history is running dead last among the names that actually matter in it.

The detail that should end the conversation came next. Because Grok demand was so weak, xAI's enormous Colossus data center sat badly underused — by one internal memo running at roughly **11% utilization** against an industry production norm of 35–45%. So SpaceX did the only rational thing with compute it could not fill: it rented it out. To whom? To **Anthropic, at a reported $1.25 billion per month, and to Google, at about $920 million per month** — together on the order of $26 billion in annual recurring revenue. The bulk of SpaceX's go-forward "AI" revenue comes not from selling AI, but from leasing its surplus GPUs to the very competitors that beat it in the market — because its own models were not good enough to keep the hardware busy. It is, quite literally, monetizing its own failure to compete in AI, and then booking the proceeds as proof of its place in a $26.5 trillion AI future. The valuation rests not on a position SpaceX occupies but on one it asserts it might someday reach: orbital data centers it has not yet launched, chip ventures not yet shipping, a 100-gigawatt compute build that exists entirely in the future tense.

I am not picking on SpaceX's space engineering, which is extraordinary. I am pointing at what the filing reveals about the *market's* state of mind. When the AI narrative is so powerful that a company holding under 2% of the enterprise AI market can reach for a $26.5 trillion AI TAM to anchor a trillion-dollar valuation, you are no longer looking at a sober estimate of demand. You are looking at a story so large it has become unfalsifiable — no company captures a majority of a $28 trillion market, so the number can never be proven wrong, which is precisely what makes it useless as a basis for a valuation and revealing as a symptom. TAM figures in S-1s are always optimistic. This one is optimistic on a scale that exceeds the country printing the prospectus, claimed by a company that has barely entered the market it's sizing. That is what the top of a cycle reads like. The dot-com filings of 1999 made their own versions of this move ("the entire economy is moving online") right before the entire economy declined to do so on the timeline the valuations required.

When a company with almost no share of a market tells you that market is bigger than the U.S. economy — and funds itself by renting picks and shovels to the competitors who are winning it — the number has stopped describing the world and started describing the mania.

## The catalyst: $400 billion of new equity is about to hit the market

Everything above is a case that the price is wrong. None of it tells you *when*. The answer (the spark, and the reason I think the clock is now measured in months rather than years) is a supply-and-demand problem that Scott Galloway and Ed Elson laid out cleanly on *Prof G Markets*, and the arithmetic is startling.

**The supply.** Look at the equity issuance about to land, all at once. SpaceX is raising roughly **$75 billion** in its IPO, already the largest in history, dwarfing the prior record (Saudi Aramco's ~$29 billion in 2019). Anthropic, fresh off a **$65 billion** private round, is expected to go public later this year and could raise on the order of $100 billion. OpenAI, which last raised **$122 billion** privately, is expected to do the same. Add Alphabet's **$85 billion** offering (itself the largest equity raise of any kind in history), and these four alone are about to ask the market for roughly **$360 billion** of fresh capital, on top of the ~$40 billion already raised in IPOs this year. Call it **$400 billion of new equity supply** arriving in a single window.

Set that against the historical baseline. The biggest IPO *year* on record was 2021, at about $140 billion; last year the figure was roughly $44 billion. The market is about to be asked to absorb something like three times its best year ever (nearly ten times last year) in a matter of months. Econ 101: when supply outstrips demand, the price falls, and equities are no exception. Investors do not have $400 billion sitting idle in cash; to buy the new paper, they have to sell something. They will not sell their houses, and they are unlikely to dump defensive positions (utilities, healthcare, industrials) to rotate into the riskiest growth stories on the board. What they will trim is the thing most correlated with what they're buying: their existing AI and tech holdings, names like Nvidia, Broadcom, the chip makers, and Tesla. Funding the new AI offerings means selling the old AI winners. That is exactly the dynamic that pulls the floor out from under the very AI equities that are now holding up the index — a concentration I'll put hard numbers to at the end.

**The cash objection.** The obvious objection is that there is plenty of cash to soak this up: U.S. money-market funds hold a record **~$7.9–8.3 trillion**, so what is $400 billion against that? Two things. First, the hoard is enormous in dollars but near a *45-year low* relative to the size of the market it would have to support — as a share of S&P 500 market capitalization, money-fund assets sit around their lowest level since 1980, and most of the pile is government and institutional operating cash, not risk-on money waiting to buy IPOs. Second, and more fundamental, "cash on the sidelines" is partly a myth: in ordinary trading, when cash buys a share it does not disappear into the market, it transfers to whoever sold — so the aggregate pile never gets "used up" lifting prices. But that is precisely why this wave is dangerous rather than reassuring. A flood of IPOs is *not* ordinary trading: new issuance creates *net-new shares* and routes the cash to the companies, who spend it on data centers and chips. So this is not really a story about cash being drained — it is a story about the **supply of equity expanding by $400 billion** into demand that is not growing nearly as fast, and supply that outruns demand clears at a lower price. A record cash balance is not the same thing as a record willingness to buy AI equities at these valuations.

The Alphabet deal contains a tell. Berkshire Hathaway took its $10 billion slice at a **6.5% discount** to the market price. Why would one of the most cash-generative companies on earth (a firm that could comfortably fund its AI capex off its own balance sheet) discount stock to lock in a marquee buyer? Galloway's read is that this is a CFO "cutting the line": grabbing the cheap capital still desperate to buy into AI before SpaceX, OpenAI, and Anthropic soak it all up. Either way, when even Alphabet has to offer a discount, it is signaling that capital (the thing this entire build depends on) is getting scarce even at the very top.

History rhymes here too. A Truist study of 30 major IPOs over the past 15 years found an **average maximum drawdown of 55% in the first year** — at some point in year one, half your money gone (Meta fell 54%, Palantir 53%, Robinhood 90%), with the median name down 9% twelve months out. Blockbuster IPOs are peak hype and peak demand by construction; they tend to mark a local top, not a launch pad. And four of the largest equity raises in history are about to price within months of each other, into the most concentrated market on record.

**The passive-money trap.** There is a second-order version of this that could amplify it: passive money. Because these companies are unprofitable, they would not qualify for the major equity indices under current rules. But if index providers loosen those rules to admit listings this large early (waiving the usual seasoning periods and profitability tests), inclusion stops being optional for the trillions tracking those benchmarks. Index funds would then have to buy the new entrants and fund it by trimming everything else they hold. Because the benchmark is already so concentrated in AI, a large share of that forced selling would land on the very AI winners holding the index up. It would be the boom rebalancing against itself.

So this is the real test — and notice that it is not a test of whether AI works. It is a test of whether the market can absorb $400 billion of AI paper at these valuations without cracking the AI trade that is holding up the entire index. My bet is that it cannot: that the act of these companies going public is itself the event most likely to mark the top, because there is not enough demand at these valuations to absorb the new supply and hold the old prices aloft at the same time.

## The strongest version of the other side

I want to be fair to the bull case, because the people making it are not fools and some of them will be right.

The optimists argue that the internet analogy cuts both ways: yes, there was a crash, but the internet *did* eat the world, and the companies that survived (Amazon, Google) became the most valuable in history. Underbuilding capacity for a genuinely revolutionary technology may be a worse error than overbuilding it, and a CEO who throttled AI spend to protect this quarter's margins could be the one who gets fired in five years. They argue that test-time compute is a brand-new scaling axis still in its infancy, that agentic systems are unlocking real economic value that the MIT-style pilot studies are too early to capture, and that today's "unprofitable" labs look exactly like Amazon did when it was plowing every dollar back into growth. They would note that AWS, Google Cloud, and Azure AI revenues are growing 30–120% year over year — that is not nothing. And they would point out, correctly, that bubbles can inflate for years longer than skeptics expect, and that being early is indistinguishable from being wrong right up until it isn't.

These are real arguments. My response is not that they are baseless but that they are bets on the long tail (on the option paying off), and that you can believe the technology transforms the economy over fifteen years while still believing the *equities are mispriced* over the next two or three. Both can be true. The internet bulls of 1999 were directionally right about the world and catastrophically wrong about the entry price. I think that is the most likely shape of this one too.

## What would change my mind

I try to hold this view as a thesis, not a religion, so here is what would disprove it. The biggest one: a genuine solution to hallucination — a model that can reliably know and signal what it does not know, trustworthy enough to operate without a human checking its work in high-stakes domains. That single development would unlock most of the applications the valuations assume, and I would have to revisit the whole thesis. A new model generation that delivers a GPT-3-to-GPT-4-sized leap in *raw base-model* capability, clearly attributable to the model and not the harness, would tell me the scaling story has a second act and the data wall was surmountable. A frontier lab demonstrating real, durable pricing power (raising prices and holding share against the open-weight tide rather than racing it to zero) would tell me a moat exists after all. Enterprise studies flipping from 95% no-return to a clear majority of deployments showing hard P&L impact would tell me the demand to fill the infrastructure is actually arriving. And hyperscalers showing real, disclosed positive ROI on deployed AI capex (not bundled cloud revenue, but returns on the AI build specifically) would tell me the capital allocation clears. If those things happen, my thesis will have been disproven.

## The index is the bubble

This is no longer a problem you can avoid by not owning the obvious AI names. The concentration has spread well beyond the Magnificent Seven (themselves now about 35% of the S&P 500 and some 70% of its economic profit). Widen the lens to the whole AI complex (the chipmakers, the hyperscalers, the data-center, power, and financing plays all riding the same thesis) and AI-related stocks now make up roughly **45% of the entire S&P 500's market value**, the largest share any single theme has ever reached. They are doing nearly all the work: AI-linked names have driven more than **80% of the index's gains in 2026**, with the rest of the market roughly flat since February. Stretch the window out and it gets worse. From May 2024 to June 2026 the S&P 500 returned about 142%, but strip out the AI stocks and that collapses to **16%**. Anyone holding a plain index fund, which is to say most people with a 401(k), is, whether they know it or not, running a heavily concentrated bet on AI capex paying off.

It reaches past the stock market into the economy itself. AI data-center construction has become a primary engine of U.S. growth, responsible for an estimated three-quarters of GDP growth in the first quarter of 2026, and by one measure contributing more to growth in 2025 than all consumer spending combined. That is what makes a correction here different from a contained sector unwind: a repricing of the AI thesis is not a tech-sector story, it is the index, the retirement account, and the GDP print all at once. Concentration on the way up is leverage on the way down.

My read, in the end, is straightforward. The technology is real and will still be here in a decade. The valuations and the cost structures built on top of it are not, and the chasm between the two is the defining risk of this cycle. The prudent path isn't to deny the technological shift, but to refuse to underwrite it at peak hysteria — especially at a moment when $700 billion a year is being spent to chase a return that, in a competitive market full of nearly free Chinese alternatives, Economics 101 rules out.

Being right about the technology and wrong about the math is the oldest trap in market history. I think a lot of very smart capital is currently walking right into it.
